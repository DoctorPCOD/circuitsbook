    Power

    1.

    How much power is supplied by the unknown element shown in Figure 1.7.1?
    🔗
    Answer.
    P = 8 mW
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Use conservation of power to calculate the power consumed by the unknown circuit element.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 10~\textrm{mW} + 3~\textrm{mW} -5~\textrm{mW} + P \amp = 0\\ P \amp = -8~\textrm{mW} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The negative sign indicates that the circuit element supplies 8 mW of power (hence the positive sign in the answer).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    A voltage source supplies 12 V and has a power consumption of 5 W. How much current is the voltage source supplying to the circuit?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use (1.4.1).
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(I = 417~\textrm{mA}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} i(t) \amp = \frac{p(t)}{v(t)} \\ \amp = \frac{5~\textrm{W}}{12~\textrm{V}}\\ \amp = 0.417~\textrm{A}\\ \amp = 417~\textrm{mA} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Which of the circuit elements shown in Figure 1.7.2 absorb power and which circuit elements deliver power? Current flows clockwise through the circuit.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Circuit elements A, B, and C absorb power. Circuit element D supplies power.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Analyze the direction of current relative to the voltage drops. Current flows from high to low potential in elements A, B, and C. Therefore elements A, B, and C absorb power. Current flows from low to high potential in element D. Therefore element D supplies power.
    🔗

    Sinusoidal Waves

    6.

    Calculate \(V_m\text{,}\) \(V_{DC}\text{,}\) \(f\text{,}\) and \(\phi\) of the function \(v(t)=3.8~\textrm{V}\cos(2\pi20t + \frac{25\pi}{180})+3~\textrm{V}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Refer to (1.5.2).
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{align*} V_m \amp = 3.8~\textrm{V}\\ V_{DC} \amp = 3~\textrm{V}\\ f \amp = 20~\textrm{Hz}\\ \phi \amp = 25~^\circ \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A sinusoidal wave takes the form given in (1.5.2), where \(f\) has a unit of Hz and \(\phi\) has a unit of rad. Carefully analyze the function to determine the values of each property.
    🔗
    The phase \(\phi\) of the wave can be converted to a unit of degrees by multiplying by the conversion factor.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \phi \amp = \left(\frac{25\pi}{180}~\textrm{rad}\right)\left(\frac{180~^\circ}{\pi~\textrm{rad}}\right)\\ \amp = 25~^\circ \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    Calculate \(V_m\text{,}\) \(V_{PP}\text{,}\) and \(T\) of the function \(v(t) = 2.19~\textrm{V}\cos(2\pi40t - \frac{20\pi}{180})+6~\textrm{V}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Refer to (1.5.2) and (1.5.4).
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{align*} V_m \amp = 2.19~\textrm{V}\\ V_{PP} \amp = 4.38~\textrm{V}\\ T \amp = 0.025~\textrm{s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A sinusoidal wave takes the form given in (1.5.2), where \(f\) has a unit of Hz. The period of the wave (\(T\)) is equal to the reciprocal of the frequency (\(f\)) as defined in (1.5.4). Use this to calculate the period.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} T \amp = \frac{1}{f}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{40~\textrm{Hz}}\\ \amp = 0.025~\textrm{s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The peak-to-peak voltage is equal to twice the amplitude of the wave.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} V_{PP} \amp = 2V_m\\ \amp = 2(2.19~\textrm{V})\\ \amp = 4.38~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8.

    Calculate \(V_m\text{,}\) \(V_{DC}\text{,}\) and \(f\) of the function shown in Figure 1.7.3.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Refer to Figure 1.5.4 and (1.5.4).
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{align*} V_m \amp = 2~\textrm{V}\\ V_{DC} \amp = 1~\textrm{V}\\ f \amp = 125~\textrm{Hz} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The sinusoidal wave voltages oscillate between −1 V and 3 V with an average value of 1 V. The amplitude is equal to the distance between equilibrium (1 V) and peak displacement (3 V).
    🔗
    \begin{align*} V_m \amp = 3~\textrm{V} - 1~\textrm{V}\\ \amp = 2~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The DC offset is equal to the average value of the waveform, which is 1 V.
    🔗
    To calculate the frequency, first calculate the period. Determine how much time it takes to complete one oscillation. This can be defined as the amount of time that passes between subsequent peaks on the graph. The first peak shown on the graph occurs at 0 s with the second peak occurring at 8 ms.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} T \amp = 8~\textrm{ms} - 0~\textrm{ms}\\ \amp = 8~\textrm{ms}\\ \amp = 0.008~\textrm{s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Then, use the relationship between period and frequency defined in (1.5.4) to calculate the frequency of the wave.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f \amp =\frac{1}{T}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{0.008~\textrm{s}}\\ \amp = 125~\textrm{Hz} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Sketch the function \(i(t) = 400~\textrm{mA}\cos(2\pi100t + \frac{45\pi}{180})+200~\textrm{mA}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Refer to Figure 1.5.4 and (1.5.4).
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Identify the waveform properties by referring to (1.5.4). (Note that as mentioned in Subsection 1.5.2, while the equation is expressed with terms representing voltage, the same equation can be used with terms representing current.)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} I_m \amp = 400~\textrm{mA}\\ I_{DC} \amp = 200~\textrm{mA}\\ f \amp = 100~\textrm{Hz}\\ \phi \amp = 45~^\circ \end{align*}
    🔗
    The minimum and maximum currents can be calculated based on the values of \(I_m\) and \(I_{DC}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} I_{max} \amp = I_{DC} + I_m\\ \amp = 200~\textrm{mA} + 400~\textrm{mA}\\ \amp = 600~\textrm{mA}\\ I_{min} \amp = I_{DC} - I_m\\ \amp = 200~\textrm{mA} - 400~\textrm{mA}\\ \amp = -200~\textrm{mA} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The first minimum will occur when the argument of the cosine function is equal to \(\pi\text{.}\) This is because \(\cos(\pi) = -1\text{,}\) which represents the minimum of the cosine function. This calculation establishes the time shift (rather than degree shift) of the function.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \pi \amp = 2\pi100t_{min} + \frac{45\pi}{180}\\ 200t_{min} \amp = 1 - \frac{45}{180}\\ t_{min} \amp = \frac{1-\frac{45}{180}}{200}\\ \amp = 0.00375~\textrm{s}\\ \amp = 3.75~\textrm{ms} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The period of the wave can be calculated by applying (1.5.4) to the argument of the cosine function.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} T \amp= \frac{1}{100~\textrm{Hz}}\\ \amp= 0.01~\textrm{s}\\ \amp= 10~\textrm{ms} \end{align*}
    🔗
    This means that each subsequent minimum will occur in intervals of 10 ms. Maxima will occur at 10 ms intervals as well, and they will be located halfway between minima.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    What minimum DC offset should be added to \(i(t) = 30~\textrm{mA}\cos(2\pi200t - \frac{50\pi}{180})\) to prevent any negative values from occurring on the output?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(I_{DC,min} = 30~\textrm{mA}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(i(t)\) to remain positive, the minimum allowable current will be 0 mA. Use the relationship between minimum value, DC offset, and amplitude to calculate this value.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} I_{min} \amp = I_{DC,min} - I_m\\ I_{DC,min} \amp = I_{min} + I_m\\ \amp = 0~\textrm{mA} + 30~\textrm{mA}\\ \amp = 30~\textrm{mA} \end{align*}
    🔗

    16.

    Derive an equation for the signal shown in Figure 1.7.12.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(f(t) = 2\delta(t+4) - \delta(t+2) - \delta(t) + 1.5\delta(t-5)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The left-most delta function in Figure 1.7.12 occurs at \(\tau = -4~\textrm{s}\) and has an amplitude of \(A = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The next delta function occurs at \(\tau = -2~\textrm{s}\) and has an amplitude of \(A = -1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The next delta function occurs at \(\tau = 0~\textrm{s}\) and has an amplitude of \(A = -1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    🔗
    At \(t = -1~\textrm{s}\text{,}\) another step function with amplitude of \(A = 1\) is added. This is equal to \(u(t+1)\text{.}\) The total amplitude of the function becomes 2 at this point (this is equal to the sum of the amplitudes of this and the previous step function).
    🔗
    At \(t = 1~\textrm{s}\text{,}\) another step function of amplitude \(A = -3\) is added. This is equal to \(-3u(t-1)\text{.}\) The total amplitude of the function becomes −1 at this point (this is equal to the sum of the amplitudes of this and the previous two step functions).

This is equal to \(u(t-4)\text{.}\) The total amplitude of the function becomes zero at this point (this is equal to the sum of the amplitudes of this and the previous three step functions). After that point, the signal remains constant at zero.
