    Section 7.5 Homogeneous Second-Order Circuits

    The solution to a homogeneous second-order differential equation is known as the general solution.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.5.1 The Overdamped Solution

    The solution to a homogeneous overdamped circuit is described in (7.5.1), with coefficients \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) defined in (7.5.2) and (7.5.3), respectively. Note that this equation is defined with voltage terms. If calculating current, all \(v(t)\) terms can be replaced by \(i(t)\) terms.
    🔗
    \begin{align} v(t) \amp= \left(A_1\textrm{e}^{s_1t} + A_2\textrm{e}^{s_2t}\right)u(t) + v(0^-)u(-t)\tag{7.5.1}\\ A_1 \amp= \frac{v(0^+)s_2-v'(0^+)}{s_2-s_1}\tag{7.5.2}\\ A_2 \amp= \frac{v'(0^+)-v(0^+)s_1}{s_2-s_1}\tag{7.5.3} \end{align}
    🔗
    Coefficients \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) will have a unit corresponding to the quantity being described by the equation. (Volts for voltage, amps or milliamps for current.) These two coefficients are derived by solving (7.5.1) at \(t=0^+\) and by solving the first derivative of (7.5.1) at \(t=0^+\text{.}\) This derivation is shown in Derivation 7.5.1.1.
    🔗

    Derivation.
    Start by solving (7.5.1) when \(t=0^+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v(0^+) \amp= A_1\textrm{e}^{s_1*0} + A_2\textrm{e}^{s_2*0}\\ v(0^+) \amp= A_1 + A_2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Calculate the first derivative of (7.5.1) and solve when \(t=0^+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dt}v(t) \amp= \frac{d}{dt}\left(A_1\textrm{e}^{s_1t} + A_2\textrm{e}^{s_2t}\right)\\ v'(t) \amp= s_1A_1\textrm{e}^{s_1t} + s_2A_2\textrm{e}^{s_2t}\\ v'(0^+) \amp= s_1A_1\textrm{e}^{s_1*0} + s_2A_2\textrm{e}^{s_2*0}\\ v'(0^+) \amp= s_1A_1 + s_2A_2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use the equations for \(v(0^+)\) and \(v'(0^+)\) to solve for \(A_1\) and \(A_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A_1 \amp= v(0^+) - A_2\\ v'(0^+) \amp= s_1(v(0^+)-A_2) + s_2A_2\\ \amp= s_1v(0^+) - A_2s_1 + A_2s_2\\ v'(0^+) - s_1v(0^+) \amp= A_2 (s_2 - s_1)\\ A_2 \amp= \frac{v'(0^+) - s_1v(0^+)}{s_2-s_1}\\ A_1 \amp= v(0^+) - \left(\frac{v'(0^+) - s_1v(0^+)}{s_2-s_1}\right)\\ A_1(s_2-s_1) \amp= v(0^+)(s_2-s_1) - v'(0^+) + s_1v(0^+)\\ \amp= v(0^+)s_2 - v(0^+)s_1 - v'(0^+) + s_1v(0^+)\\ \amp= v(0^+)s_2 - v'(0^+)\\ A_1 \amp= \frac{v(0^+)s_2 - v'(0^+)}{s_2-s_1} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.5.2 The Underdamped Solution

    The solution to a homogeneous underdamped circuit is described in (7.5.4), with coefficients \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) defined in (7.5.5) and (7.5.6), respectively. Note that this equation is defined with voltage terms. If calculating current, all \(v(t)\) terms can be replaced by \(i(t)\) terms.
    🔗
    \begin{align} v(t) \amp= \textrm{e}^{-\alpha t} \left[ B_1\cos{\beta t} + B_2\sin{\beta t} \right]u(t) + v(0^-)u(-t)\tag{7.5.4}\\ B_1 \amp= v(0^+)\tag{7.5.5}\\ B_2 \amp= \frac{v'(0^+)+\alpha v(0^+)}{\beta}\tag{7.5.6} \end{align}
    🔗
    Coefficients \(B_1\) and \(B_2\) will have a unit corresponding to the quantity being described by the equation. (Volts for voltage, amps or milliamps for current.) These two coefficients are derived by solving (7.5.4) at \(t=0^+\) and by solving the first derivative of (7.5.4) at \(t=0^+\text{.}\) This derivation is shown in Derivation 7.5.2.1.
    🔗

    Derivation.
    Start by solving (7.5.4) when \(t=0^+\text{.}\) This conveniently derives coefficient \(B_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v(0^+) \amp= \textrm{e}^{-\alpha*0} \left[ B_1\cos{(\beta *0)} + B_2\sin{(\beta *0)} \right]\\ v(0^+) \amp= B_1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Calculate the first derivative of (7.5.4) and solve when \(t=0^+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dt}v(t) \amp= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\textrm{e}^{-\alpha t} \left[ B_1\cos{\beta t} + B_2\sin{\beta t} \right]\right)\\ v'(t) \amp= \textrm{e}^{-\alpha t}\left[(\cos{\beta t})(B_2\beta - \alpha B_1) - (\sin{\beta t})(\alpha B_2 + \beta B_1)\right]\\ v'(0^+) \amp= \textrm{e}^{-\alpha *0}\left[(\cos{(\beta *0)})(B_2\beta - \alpha B_1) - (\sin{(\beta *0)})(\alpha B_2 + \beta B_1)\right]\\ v'(0^+) \amp= B_2\beta - \alpha B_1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use the equations for \(v(0^+)\) and \(v'(0^+)\) to solve for \(B_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v'(0^+) \amp= B_2\beta - \alpha B_1\\ v'(0^+) + \alpha B_1 \amp= B_2\beta\\ B_2 \amp= \frac{v'(0^+) + \alpha B_1}{\beta}\\ B_2 \amp= \frac{v'(0^+) + \alpha v(0^+)}{\beta} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.5.3 The Critically Damped Solution

    The solution to a homogeneous critically damped circuit is described in (7.5.7), with coefficients \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) defined in (7.5.8) and (7.5.9), respectively. Note that this equation is defined with voltage terms. If calculating current, all \(v(t)\) terms can be replaced by \(i(t)\) terms.
    🔗
    \begin{align} v(t) \amp= \left(A_1\textrm{e}^{-\alpha t} + A_2t\textrm{e}^{-\alpha t}\right)u(t) + v(0^-)u(-t)\tag{7.5.7}\\ A_1 \amp= v(0^+)\tag{7.5.8}\\ A_2 \amp= v'(0^+) + \alpha v(0^+)\tag{7.5.9} \end{align}
    🔗
    Coefficient \(A_1\) has a unit corresponding to the quantity being described by the equation. (Volts for voltage, amps or milliamps for current.) Coefficient \(A_2\) has a unit corresponding to the first derivative of the quantity being described by the equation (volts per second for voltage, or amps per second or milliamps per second for current). These two coefficients are derived by solving (7.5.7) at \(t=0^+\) and by solving the first derivative of (7.5.7) at \(t=0^+\text{.}\) This derivation is shown in Derivation 7.5.3.1.
    🔗

    Derivation.
    Start by solving (7.5.7) when \(t=0^+\text{.}\) This conveniently derives coefficient \(A_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v(0^+) \amp= A_1\textrm{e}^{-\alpha *0} + A_2t\textrm{e}^{-\alpha *0}\\ v(0^+) \amp= A_1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Calculate the first derivative of (7.5.7) and solve when \(t=0^+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dt}v(t) \amp= \frac{d}{dt}\left(A_1\textrm{e}^{-\alpha t} + A_2t\textrm{e}^{-\alpha t}\right)\\ v'(t) \amp= -\alpha A_1\textrm{e}^{-\alpha t} + \textrm{e}^{-\alpha t}(A_2 - A_2\alpha t)\\ v'(0^+) \amp= -\alpha A_1\textrm{e}^{-\alpha *0} + \textrm{e}^{-\alpha *0}(A_2 - A_2\alpha *0)\\ v'(0^+) \amp= -\alpha A_1 + A_2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use the equations for \(v(0^+)\) and \(v'(0^+)\) to solve for \(A_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v'(0^+) \amp= -\alpha A_1 + A_2\\ v'(0^+) + \alpha A_1 \amp= A_2\\ A_2 \amp= v'(0^+) + \alpha v(0^+) \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.5.4 Examples

    Example 7.5.1 Homogeneous RLC series circuit.

    Derive an equation for \(v(t)\) for the circuit shown below. The switch is closed for a very long time before opening at a time of zero seconds.
    🔗
    First, simplify the circuit by combining the two capacitors in parallel.
    🔗
    Analyze the initial steady-state conditions of the circuit. The inductor can be treated as a short and the capacitor can be treated as an open.
    🔗
    Use Ohm’s law to calculate the initial current through the inductor.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i_L(0) \amp= \frac{20\textrm{V}}{320~\Omega}\\ \amp= 62.5~\textrm{mA} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use the voltage divider rule to calculate the initial voltage drop over the capacitor.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v(0) \amp= 20~\textrm{V}\left(\frac{220~\Omega}{320~\Omega}\right)\\ \amp= 13.75~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Analyze the circuit at \(t=0^+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Reduce the circuit by combining the two series resistors.
    🔗
    Because the capacitor and inductor are in series in this circuit configuration, the current through the capacitor at \(t=0^+\) will be equal to the negative value of the current through the inductor at \(t=0\text{.}\) (The value is negative because the current directions are opposing. Current through the capacitor is defined as flowing from high to low potential.)
    🔗
    Use (7.3.5) to calculate the initial first derivative of the voltage. Use base units (farads and amps) to ensure that the result has a unit of V/s.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v'(0^+) \amp= \frac{-0.0625~\textrm{A}}{940\times10^{-9}~\textrm{F}}\\ \amp= -66489.36~\textrm{V/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.3.3) to calculate the damping parameter. Use base units (henrys, ohms) to ensure that the result has a unit of Np/s.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \alpha \amp= \frac{267~\Omega}{2\times0.05~\textrm{H}}\\ \amp= 2670~\textrm{Np/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.3.4) to calculate the resonant frequency. Use base units (henrys, farads) to ensure that the result has a unit of rad/s.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_0 \amp= \frac{1}{\sqrt{(0.05~\textrm{H})(940\times10^{-9}~\textrm{F})}}\\ \amp= 4612.66~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    This circuit is underdamped. Use (7.2.5) to calculate the oscillation frequency of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \beta \amp= \sqrt{4612.66^2 - 2670^2}\\ \amp= 3761.35~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.5.5) and (7.5.6) to calculate the coefficients.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} B_1 \amp= 13.75~\textrm{V}\\ B_2 \amp= \frac{-66489.36+(2670)(13.75)}{3761.35}\\ \amp= -7.92~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Plug all of these quantities into (7.5.4) to calculate the equation for \(v(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v(t) \amp= \textrm{e}^{-2670 t} \left[13.75~\textrm{V}~\cos(3761.35 t) -7.92~\textrm{V}~\sin(3761.35 t) \right]~u(t)\\ \amp+ 13.75~\textrm{V}~u(-t) \end{align*}

    Example 7.5.14 Homogeneous parallel RLC circuit with parasitic resistance.

    Derive an equation for \(i(t)\) for the circuit shown below. The switch is closed for a very long time before opening at a time of zero seconds. The inductor has a parasitic resistance of 60 Ω, which is not explicitly shown on the circuit diagram.
    🔗

    🔗
    Use the current divider rule to calculate \(i(0)\text{.}\) This quantity is continuous with time.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i(0) \amp= 200~\textrm{mA}\left(\frac{60~\Omega//47~\Omega}{60~\Omega}\right)\\ \amp= 87.85~\textrm{mA} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use Ohm’s law to calculate \(v(0)\text{.}\) This quantity is also continuous with time. There will be no need to analyze the circuit at \(t=0^+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v(0) \amp= (0.2~\textrm{A})(60~\Omega//47~\Omega)\\ \amp= 5.27~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.4.12) to calculate the initial first derivative of the current.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i'(0) \amp= \frac{5.27~\textrm{V} - (0.08785~\textrm{A})(60~\Omega)}{0.1~\textrm{H}}\\ \amp= 0~\textrm{A/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.4.10) to calculate the damping parameter.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \alpha \amp= \frac{1}{2(47~\Omega)(22\times10^{-9}\textrm{F})}+\frac{60~\Omega}{2(0.1~\textrm{H})}\\ \amp= 483858.99~\textrm{Np/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.4.11) to calculate the resonant frequency.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_0 \amp= \sqrt{\frac{1}{(0.1~\textrm{H})(22\times10^{-9}~\textrm{F})}+\frac{60~\Omega}{(47~\Omega)(0.1~\textrm{H})(22\times10^{-9}\textrm{F})}}\\ \amp= 32168.56~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    This circuit is overdamped. Use (7.2.3) and (7.2.4) to calculate the roots of the characteristic equation.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} s_1 \amp= -483858.99 + \sqrt{483858.99^2 - 32168.56^2}\\ \amp= -1070.52\\ s_2 \amp= -483858.99 - \sqrt{483858.99^2 - 32168.56^2}\\ \amp= -966647.46 \end{align*}

    Use (7.5.2) (7.5.3) to calculate the coefficients of the equation.

    Plug all of these quantities into (7.5.1) to derive the equation for \(i(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i(t) \amp= \left(87.95~\textrm{mA}~\textrm{e}^{-1070.52t} - 0.10~\textrm{mA}~\textrm{e}^{-966647.46t}\right)~u(t)\\ \amp+ 87.85~\textrm{mA}~u(-t) \end{align*}

    Example 7.5.18 Homogeneous general second-order circuit.

    Derive an equation for \(i(t)\) for the circuit shown below. The switch is closed for a very long time before opening at a time of zero seconds. The circuit parameters are: \(R_1 = 20~\Omega\text{,}\) \(R_2 = 30~\Omega\text{,}\) \(R_3 = 30~\Omega\text{,}\) \(L_1 = 500~\textrm{mH}\text{,}\) and \(L_2 = 400~\textrm{mH}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Analyze the circuit in its initial steady-state configuration Both inductors can be replaced by shorts.
    🔗
    Transform the voltage source into a current source.
    🔗
    Calculate the equivalent resistance of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} R_{EQ} \amp= 20~\Omega // R_1 // R_2 // R_3\\ \amp= 20~\Omega // 20~\Omega // 30~\Omega // 30~\Omega\\ \amp= 6~\Omega \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use the current divider rule to calculate \(i(0)\text{.}\) This value will be continuous with time.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i(0) \amp= 0.5~\textrm{A}\left(\frac{6~\Omega}{30~\Omega}\right)\\ \amp= 0.1~\textrm{A} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use KCL to calculate \(i_X(0)\text{.}\) This value will also be continuous with time. Because both \(R_2\) and \(R_3\) are 30 Ω resistors, they will have equal current values in the initial steady-state condition.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i_X(0) \amp= 0.1~\textrm{A} + 0.1~\textrm{A}\\ \amp= 0.2~\textrm{A} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use Ohm’s law to calculate \(v_X(0^-)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v_X(0^-) \amp= (0.1~\textrm{A})(30~\Omega)\\ \amp= 3~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    \(v_X(t)\) may not be continuous with time. Analyze the circuit at \(t=0^+\) to calculate \(v_X(0^+)\text{.}\) Replace \(L_1\) with a 0.2 A current source. Replace \(L_2\) with a 0.1 A current source.
    🔗
    From KCL, we can see that the current through \(R_2\) will be equal to 0.1 A. Therefore, \(v_X(0^+) = 3~\textrm{V}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Analyze the circuit at \(t=0^+\) to derive the second-order differential equation.
    🔗
    Perform KCL at the node connecting \(L_1\text{,}\) \(R_2\text{,}\) and \(R_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} i_X(t) = \frac{v_X(t)}{R_2} + i(t)\tag{7.5.10} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Perform KVL around the left-hand loop of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} v_X(t) = -L_1\frac{di_X(t)}{dt} - R_1i_X(t)\tag{7.5.11} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Plug (7.5.10) into (7.5.11) to remove \(i_X(t)\) terms.
    🔗
    \begin{align} v_X(t) \amp= -L_1\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{v_X(t)}{R_2} + i(t)\right) - R_1\left(\frac{v_X(t)}{R_2} + i(t)\right)\tag{7.5.12}\\ \amp= -\frac{L_1}{R_2}\frac{dv_X(t)}{dt} - L_1\frac{di(t)}{dt} - \frac{R_1}{R_2}v_X(t) - R_1i(t)\tag{7.5.13}\\ 0 \amp= v_X(t) + \frac{R_1}{R_2}v_X(t) + \frac{L_1}{R_2}\frac{dv_X(t)}{dt} + L_1\frac{di(t)}{dt} + R_1i(t)\tag{7.5.14}\\ \amp= \left(1 + \frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)v_X(t) + \frac{L_1}{R_2}\frac{dv_X(t)}{dt} + L_1\frac{di(t)}{dt} + R_1i(t)\tag{7.5.15} \end{align}
    🔗
    Perform KVL around the right-hand loop of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} v_X(t) = R_3i(t) + L_2\frac{di(t)}{dt}\tag{7.5.16} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Plug (7.5.16) into (7.5.15) and reduce to standard form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 0 \amp= \left(1 + \frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)\left(R_3i(t) + L_2\frac{di(t)}{dt}\right) + \frac{L_1}{R_2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(R_3i(t) + L_2\frac{di(t)}{dt}\right)\\ \amp+ L_1\frac{di(t)}{dt} + R_1i(t)\\ \amp= R_3i(t) + L_2\frac{di(t)}{dt} + \frac{R_1R_3}{R_2}i(t) + \frac{L_2R_1}{R_2}\frac{di(t)}{dt} + \frac{L_1R_3}{R_2}\frac{di(t)}{dt}\\ \amp+ \frac{L_1L_2}{R_2}\frac{d^2i(t)}{dt^2} + L_1\frac{di(t)}{dt} + R_1i(t)\\ \amp= \frac{L_1L_2}{R_2}\frac{d^2i(t)}{dt^2} + \left(L_2 + \frac{L_2R_1}{R_2} + \frac{L_1R_3}{R_2} + L_1\right)\frac{di(t)}{dt}\\ \amp+ \left(R_3 + \frac{R_1R_3}{R_2} + R_1\right)i(t)\\ \amp= \frac{d^2i(t)}{dt^2} + \left(\frac{R_2}{L_1} + \frac{R_1}{L_1} + \frac{R_3}{L_2} + \frac{R_2}{L_2}\right)\frac{di(t)}{dt}\\ \amp+ \left(\frac{R_2R_3}{L_1L_2} + \frac{R_1R_3}{L_1L_2} + \frac{R_1R_2}{L_1L_2}\right)i(t) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Plug in component values using base units.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0 = \frac{d^2i(t)}{dt^2} + 250\frac{di(t)}{dt} + 10500i(t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Use (7.2.1) to calculate the damping parameter and (7.2.2) to calculate the resonant frequency.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \alpha \amp= \frac{250}{2}\\ \amp= 125~\textrm{Np/s}\\ \omega_0 \amp= \sqrt{10500}\\ \amp= 102.47~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    This circuit is overdamped.
    🔗
    Use (7.5.16) to calculate the value of \(i'(0^+)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i'(0^+) \amp= \frac{v_X(0^+) - R_3i(0^+)}{L_2}\\ \amp= \frac{3~\textrm{V} - (0.1~\textrm{A})(30~\Omega)}{0.4~\textrm{H}}\\ \amp= 0~\textrm{A/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.2.3) and (7.2.4) to calculate the roots of the characteristic equation.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} s_1 \amp= -125 + \sqrt{125^2 - 102.47^2}\\ \amp= -53.41\\ s_2 \amp= -125 - \sqrt{125^2 - 102.47^2}\\ \amp= -196.59 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (7.5.2) and (7.5.3) to calculate the coefficients of the equation.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A_1 \amp= \frac{(0.1)(-196.59)}{-196.59+53.41}\\ \amp= 0.14~\textrm{A}\\ A_2 \amp= \frac{-(0.1)(-53.41)}{-196.59+53.41}\\ \amp= -0.04~\textrm{A} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Plug all of these quantities into (7.5.1) to obtain the equation for i(t).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i(t) = \left(0.14~\textrm{A}~\textrm{e}^{-53.41t} - 0.04~\textrm{A}~\textrm{e}^{-196.59t}\right)~u(t) + 0.1~\textrm{A}~u(-t) \end{equation*}
