    Section 8.2 Impedance

    Impedance is the phasor equivalent of resistance and is used in AC circuits. It relates to the measure of opposition to current based on a given voltage rise or drop. The unit of impedance is the same as that of resistance, Ω (ohm).
    🔗

    Remark 8.2.1 Summary of impedance.

    • Symbol: \(\vec{Z}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Unit: Ω (ohm)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Using time-varying signals, \(z(t) = v(t)/i(t)\text{.}\) Using phasors, the relationship between voltage, current, and impedance is defined by complex Ohm’s law, defined in (8.2.1).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{Z} = \frac{\vec{V}}{\vec{I}}\tag{8.2.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Because impedance is a complex quantity, it can be represented in cartesian form, shown in (8.2.2). The quantity \(R\) is the resistance of the circuit element (this is the same resistance that we’ve been discussing in this textbook since Section 2.2), and \(X\) is the reactance of the circuit element.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{Z} = R + jX\tag{8.2.2} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Remark 8.2.2 Summary of reactance.

    • Symbol: \(X\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Unit: Ω (ohm)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.1 Impedance in Resistors

    In a resistor, voltage and current are related via Ohm’s law (introduced in Section 2.4). The impedance of a resistor is defined in (8.2.3), where \(R\) is the resistance of the resistor. (There is no difference in the way we express resistance in AC circuits as compared to DC circuits.)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{Z} = R\tag{8.2.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    If the voltage drop over a resistor is defined as \(V_m\cos(\omega t + \phi)\text{,}\) then Ohm’s law states that the current through the resistor will be equal to \((V_m/R)\cos(\omega t +\phi)\text{.}\) The calculation of the impedance of a resistor is derived in Derivation 8.2.1.1.
    🔗

    Derivation.
    \begin{align*} \vec{V} \amp=V_m \angle \phi\\ \vec{I} \amp= \frac{V_m}{R} \angle \phi\\ \vec{Z} \amp= \frac{\vec{V}}{\vec{I}}\\ \amp= \frac{V_m \angle \phi}{(V_m/R)\angle \phi}\\ \amp= R \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.2 Impedance in Capacitors

    The impedance of a capacitor is defined in (8.2.4), where \(C\) is the capacitance of the capacitor.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{Z} = \frac{1}{j \omega C}\tag{8.2.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Because \(j\) is in the denominator of the impedance equation, the reactance of a capacitor will always be negative.
    🔗
    The impedance of a capacitor is purely imaginary (mathematically) and is frequency dependent. As the frequency of a circuit approaches zero, the impedance of a capacitor approaches infinity. This explains why we were able to analyze capacitors in steady-state DC conditions as open circuits. As the frequency of a circuit approaches infinity, the impedance of a capacitor becomes zero, making it act as a short circuit.
    🔗
    In a capacitor, the relationship between voltage and current is defined by (5.3.1). The calculation of the impedance of a capacitor is derived in Derivation 8.2.2.1.
    🔗

    Derivation.
    \begin{align*} i(t) \amp= C\frac{dv(t)}{dt}\\ \vec{I} \amp= C \frac{d}{dt}\vec{V}\\ \amp= j\omega C \vec{V}\\ \vec{Z} \amp= \frac{\vec{V}}{\vec{I}}\\ \amp= \frac{\vec{V}}{j\omega C \vec{V}}\\ \amp= \frac{1}{j \omega C} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.3 Impedance in Inductors

    The impedance of an inductor is defined in (8.2.5), where \(L\) is the inductance of the inductor.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{Z} = j\omega L\tag{8.2.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Because \(j\) is in the numerator of the impedance equation, the reactance of an inductor will always be positive.
    🔗
    The impedance of an inductor is purely imaginary (mathematically) and is frequency dependent. As the frequency of a circuit approaches zero, the impedance of an inductor also approaches zero. This explains why we were able to analyze inductors in steady-state DC conditions as short circuits. As the frequency of a circuit approaches infinity, the impedance of an inductor also approaches infinity, making it act as an open circuit.
    🔗
    In an inductor, the relationship between voltage and current is defined by (6.3.1). The calculation of the impedance of an inductor is derived in Derivation 8.2.3.1.
    🔗

    Derivation.
    \begin{align*} v(t) \amp= L\frac{di(t)}{dt}\\ \vec{V} \amp= L \frac{d}{dt}\vec{I}\\ \amp= j\omega L \vec{I}\\ \vec{Z} \amp= \frac{\vec{V}}{\vec{I}}\\ \amp= \frac{j\omega L \vec{I}}{\vec{I}}\\ \amp= j \omega L \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.4 Equivalent Impedance

    Impedance takes a lot of confusion out of simplifying circuits. Impedances in series always add together, as shown in (8.2.6).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{Z_{EQ}} = \vec{Z_1} + \vec{Z_2} + \vec{Z_3} + ... + \vec{Z_n}\tag{8.2.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The reciprocal sum is used to calculate parallel impedances, as shown in (8.2.7).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{\vec{Z_{EQ}}} = \frac{1}{\vec{Z_1}} + \frac{1}{\vec{Z_2}} + \frac{1}{\vec{Z_3}} + ... + \frac{1}{\vec{Z_n}}\tag{8.2.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Deriving equivalent impedance enables us to simplify circuits containing resistors, capacitors, and inductors, which we were unable to do in previous chapters.
    🔗

    Example 8.2.3 Equivalent impedance.

    Calculate the equivalent impedance of the circuit shown below, and express the simplified circuit with individual components. The frequency of the circuit is 100 Hz.
    🔗
    Calculate the impedance of the 70 µF capacitor.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{j 2\pi100 (70\times10^{-6})} = -j22.74~\Omega \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Calculate the impedance of the 65 µF capacitor.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{j 2\pi100 (65\times10^{-6})} = -j24.49~\Omega \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Calculate the impedance of the inductor.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} j2\pi100(0.065) = j40.84~\Omega \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Re-draw the circuit.
    🔗
    Combine all series impedances. The 20 Ω resistor is in series with the −j22.74 Ω capacitor. The 50 Ω resistor is in series with the −j24.49 Ω capacitor.
    🔗
    Two of the impedances (j40.84 Ω and 50−j24.49 Ω) are connected in parallel.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{(j40.84~\Omega)(50-j24.49~\Omega)}{(j40.84~\Omega)+(50-j24.49~\Omega)} = 30.13+j30.98~\Omega \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The last two impedances can be combined in series to an equivalent impedance of 50.13+j8.25 Ω. This impedance consists of a resistor (50.1 Ω) and an inductor. (We know it must be an inductor because the reactance is positive.) The value of that inductor can be calculated using (8.2.5).
    🔗
    \begin{align*} L \amp= \frac{8.25}{2\pi100}\\ \amp= 13.1~\textrm{mH} \end{align*}
