    Section 8.1 Phasor Arithmetic

    It is my strong opinion that a calculator should be used to solve phasor arithmetic and complex algebra, as there are many steps that can go wrong when calculating by hand. However, it is my even stronger opinion that a calculator or other tool should never be used if the user doesn’t understand how to perform those operations themselves. Having a solid understanding of carrying out these complex arithmetic operations by hand is essential in performing “smell tests” on the calculator’s results, among other things.
    🔗
    Before discussing how to perform complex arithmetic, a review of Cartesian and polar forms will be conducted.
    🔗
    Cartesian coordinates are used to define the location of a point on the imaginary axes using two quantities: the real part and the imaginary part. This is similar to \((x,y)\) coordinates; the \(x\)-coordinate corresponds to the real part of a complex number and the \(y\)-coordinate corresponds to the imaginary part of the complex number.
    🔗
    Polar coordinates are used to define the location of a point using the magnitude of the vector (distance from the origin) and the angle from the positive real axis. Cartesian and polar representations of the point \((x,y)\) are depicted in Figure 8.1.1.
    🔗
    To precisely calculate the angle of a phasor, the two-argument arctan function (atan2) should be used, as arctan alone is unable to determine in which quadrant a point is located. The atan2 function is defined in (8.1.1).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \atantwo(y,x) = \begin{cases} \atan(\frac{y}{x}) \amp \textrm{if}~x \gt 0, \\ \atan(\frac{y}{x}) + \pi \amp \textrm{if}~x \lt 0~\textrm{and}~y \geq 0,\\ \atan(\frac{y}{x}) - \pi \amp \textrm{if}~x \lt 0~\textrm{and}~y \lt 0,\\ +\frac{\pi}{2} \amp \textrm{if}~x = 0~\textrm{and}~y \gt 0,\\ -\frac{\pi}{2} \amp \textrm{if}~x = 0~\textrm{and}~y \lt 0,\\ \textrm{undefined} \amp \textrm{if}~x=0~\textrm{and}~y=0 \end{cases}\tag{8.1.1} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.1 Cartesian to Polar Conversion

    If the Cartesian form (\(x +jy\)) of a phasor is known, it is possible to use trigonometry to represent the phasor in polar form as \(r \angle \phi\text{.}\) The magnitude \(r\) is calculated using the Pythagorean theorem, shown in (8.1.2).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\tag{8.1.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The angle \(\phi\) is calculated using the atan2 function, described in (8.1.3).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \phi = \atantwo(y,x)\tag{8.1.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.2 Polar to Cartesian Conversion

    If the polar form (\(r\angle \phi\)) of a phasor is known, it is possible to use trigonometry to express it in Cartesian form as \(x+jy\text{.}\) The real part of the phasor, expressed as \(x\text{,}\) is calculated using the cosine of the angle, shown in (8.1.4).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} x = r\cos{\phi}\tag{8.1.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The imaginary part of the phasor, expressed as \(y\text{,}\) is calculated using the sine of the angle, shown in (8.1.5).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} y = r\sin{\phi}\tag{8.1.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.3 Phasor Addition and Subtraction

    Phasor addition and subtraction is simplest to complete when the phasors are in Cartesian form. To add two phasors together, simply add the real parts together to find the real part of the sum and add the imaginary parts together to find the imaginary part of the sum. The solution to \(\vec{Z_1} + \vec{Z_2}\) is shown in (8.1.8).
    🔗
    \begin{align} \vec{Z_1} \amp= x_1 + jy_1\tag{8.1.6}\\ \vec{Z_2} \amp= x_2 + jy_2\tag{8.1.7}\\ \vec{Z_1} + \vec{Z_2} \amp= (x_1+x_2) + j(y_1+y_2)\tag{8.1.8} \end{align}
    🔗

    Example 8.1.2 Addition of two sinusoids.

    Add together \(v_1(t)\) and \(v_2(t)\text{,}\) defined below, and express the answer in cosine form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v_1(t) \amp= 10~\textrm{V}~\cos(2\pi100t+30\pi/180)\\ v_2(t) \amp= -5~\textrm{V}~\sin(2\pi100t-45\pi/180) \end{align*}
    🔗
    First, use (8.0.4) to convert \(v_2(t)\) to standard form.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_2(t) = 5~\textrm{V}~\cos(2\pi100t+45\pi/180) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Both of the quantities can be expressed as phasors in Cartesian form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \vec{V_1} \amp= 10~\textrm{V} \angle 30^\circ \amp= 8.66 +j5~\textrm{V}\\ \vec{V_2} \amp= 5~\textrm{V} \angle 45^\circ \amp= 3.54 +j3.54~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Add both of the phasors together.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \vec{V_1} + \vec{V_2} \amp= (8.66 + 3.54) + j(5 + 3.54)~\textrm{V}\\ \amp= 12.2 + j8.54~\textrm{V} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (8.1.2) and (8.1.3) to convert the result to polar form.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{V_1} + \vec{V_2} = 14.9~\textrm{V} \angle 35.0^\circ \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Express the result as a cosine.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_t(t) + v_2(t) = 14.9~\textrm{V}~\cos(2\pi100t + 35.0\pi/180) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Subtraction is very similar to addition. However, it is important to note that subtraction, unlike addition, is not commutative. The solution to \(\vec{Z_1} - \vec{Z_2}\) is shown in (8.1.11).
    🔗
    \begin{align} \vec{Z_1} \amp= x_1 + jy_1\tag{8.1.9}\\ \vec{Z_2} \amp= x_2 + jy_2\tag{8.1.10}\\ \vec{Z_1} - \vec{Z_2} \amp= (x_1-x_2) + j(y_1-y_2)\tag{8.1.11} \end{align}
    🔗

    Example 8.1.3 Subtraction of two sinusoids.

    Subtract \(i_2(t)\) from \(i_1(t)\text{,}\) defined below, and express the answer in cosine form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i_1(t) \amp= 120~\textrm{mA}~\cos(2\pi60t+60\pi/180)\\ i_2(t) \amp= 110~\textrm{mA}~\cos(2\pi60t-135\pi/180) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Both of the quantities can be expressed as phasors in Cartesian form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \vec{I_1} \amp= 120~\textrm{mA} \angle 60^\circ \amp= 60 + j103.92~\textrm{mA}\\ \vec{I_2} \amp= 110~\textrm{mA} \angle -135^\circ \amp= -77.78 - j77.78~\textrm{mA} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Subtract the phasors.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \vec{I_1} - \vec{I_2} \amp= (60 - (-77.78)) + j(103.92 - (-77.78))~\textrm{mA}\\ \amp= 137.78 + j181.70~\textrm{mA} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (8.1.2) and (8.1.3) to convert the result to polar form.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{I_1} - \vec{I_2} = 228~\textrm{mA} \angle 52.8^\circ \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Express the result as a cosine.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i_1(t) - i_2(t) = 228~\textrm{mA}~\cos(2\pi60t + 52.8\pi/180) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.4 Phasor Multiplication and Division

    Phasor multiplication and division is simplest to compute when phasors are in polar form. Recall that polar form (\(r\angle \phi\)) is shorthand for \(r\textrm{e}^{j\phi}\text{.}\) Multiplication and division in exponentials turns into addition and subtraction.
    🔗
    Two phasors \(\vec{Z_1}\) and \(\vec{Z_2}\) are multiplied together in (8.1.14).
    🔗
    \begin{align} \vec{Z_1} \amp= r_1 \angle \phi_1\tag{8.1.12}\\ \vec{Z_2} \amp= r_2 \angle \phi_2\tag{8.1.13}\\ \vec{Z_1}\vec{Z_2} \amp= r_1r_2\angle(\phi_1+\phi_2)\tag{8.1.14} \end{align}
    🔗

    Example 8.1.4 Multiplication of two sinusoids.

    Multiply \(i(t)\) and \(z(t)\text{,}\) defined below, and express the answer in cosine form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} i(t) \amp= 4~\textrm{mA}~\cos(2\pi50t+120\pi/180)\\ z(t) \amp= 20~\textrm{k}\Omega~\cos(2\pi50t-90\pi/180) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Both of the quantities can be expressed as phasors in polar form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \vec{I} \amp= 4~\textrm{mA} \angle 120^\circ\\ \vec{Z} \amp= 20~\textrm{k}\Omega \angle -90^\circ \end{align*}
    🔗
    Multiply the phasors.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \vec{I}\vec{Z} \amp= (4~\textrm{mA})(20~\textrm{k}\Omega)~\textrm{e}^{j(120^\circ + (-90^\circ))}\\ \amp= 80~\textrm{V}~\textrm{e}^{j30^\circ}\\ \amp= 80~\textrm{V} \angle 30^\circ \end{align*}
    🔗
    Express the result as a cosine. Note that the result represents a voltage and will be expressed as \(v(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v(t) = 80~\textrm{V}~\cos(2\pi50t + 30\pi/180) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Two phasors in polar form are divided in (8.1.17). It is important to note that division, unlike multiplication, is not commutative.
    🔗
    \begin{align} \vec{Z_1} \amp= r_1 \angle \phi_1\tag{8.1.15}\\ \vec{Z_2} \amp= r_2 \angle \phi_2\tag{8.1.16}\\ \frac{\vec{Z_1}}{\vec{Z_2}} \amp= \frac{r_1}{r_2}\angle(\phi_1-\phi_2)\tag{8.1.17} \end{align}
    🔗

    Example 8.1.5 Division of two sinusoids.

    Divide \(v(t)\) by \(i(t)\text{,}\) defined below, and express the answer in cosine form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} v(t) \amp= 110~\textrm{V}~\cos(2\pi60t+60\pi/180)\\ i(t) \amp= 5~\textrm{mA}~\cos(2\pi60t+30\pi/180) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Both of the quantities can be expressed as phasors in polar form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \vec{V} \amp= 110~\textrm{V} \angle 60^\circ\\ \vec{I} \amp= 5~\textrm{mA} \angle 30^\circ \end{align*}
    🔗
    Divide the phasors.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{\vec{V}}{\vec{I}} \amp= \frac{110~\textrm{V}}{5~\textrm{mA}}~\textrm{e}^{j(60^\circ - 30^\circ)}\\ \amp= 22~\textrm{k}\Omega~\textrm{e}^{j30^\circ}\\ \amp= 22~\textrm{k}\Omega \angle 30^\circ \end{align*}
    🔗
    Express the result as a cosine. Note that the result represents a quantity known as impedance (which will be introduced in Section 8.2) and will be expressed as \(z(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z(t) = 22~\textrm{k}\Omega~\cos(2\pi60t + 30\pi/180) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.5 Phasor Time-Differentiation and Time-Integration

    Time differentiation of a phasor \(\vec{Z}\) is defined in (8.1.18) and derived in Derivation 8.1.5.1.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{d}{dt}\vec{Z} = j \omega \vec{Z}\tag{8.1.18} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Derivation.
    \begin{align*} z(t) \amp= Z_m \cos{(\omega t + \phi)}\\ \vec{Z} \amp= Z_m~\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\\ \frac{d}{dt}\vec{Z} \amp= \frac{d}{dt} \left(Z_m~\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\right)\\ \amp= Z_m \frac{d}{dt} \left(\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\right)\\ \amp= j\omega Z_m~\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\\ \amp= j\omega \vec{Z} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Time integration of a phasor \(\vec{Z}\) is defined in (8.1.19) and derived in Derivation 8.1.5.2.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{d}{dt}\vec{Z} = \frac{1}{j \omega} \vec{Z}\tag{8.1.19} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Derivation.
    \begin{align*} z(t) \amp= Z_m \cos{(\omega t + \phi)}\\ \vec{Z} \amp= Z_m~\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\\ \int \vec{Z} \,dt \amp= \int \left(Z_m~\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\right)\,dt \\ \amp= Z_m \int \left(\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\right)\,dt \\ \amp= \frac{1}{j\omega} Z_m~\textrm{e}^{j(\omega t + \phi)}\\ \amp= \frac{1}{j\omega} \vec{Z} \end{align*}
    🔗
