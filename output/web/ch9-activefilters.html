    Section 9.4 Active Filters

    It is possible to design filters that use an op-amp; these filters are known as active filters. Active filters are capable of generating output amplification (from the op-amp gain). While there are many different types of active filters, only a few will be discussed in this book.
    🔗
    All properties of active filters, such as cutoff frequency, bandwidth, quality factor, and so on, are defined as discussed in Section 9.3. When calculating the cutoff frequency of an active filter, be sure to divide (normalize) by the gain of the op-amp circuit to calculate the frequency at which the magnitude of the transfer function is equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection 9.4.1 First-Order Active Filters

    First-order active filters are first-order (one energy storage element) filters that use an op-amp.
    🔗

    Example 9.4.1 Active first-order low-pass filter.

    An active first-order low-pass filter is shown below. Calculate the cutoff frequency of the filter. Then, confirm the filter type of the circuit.
    🔗
    The transfer function for this circuit was derived in Example 9.2.5 and is given below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{-\frac{1}{R_1C}}{j\omega + \frac{1}{R_2C}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To calculate the cutoff frequency, divide the transfer function by the gain of the circuit in the pass-band. Because this is a low-pass filter, the pass-band encompasses low frequencies. At low frequencies, the capacitor can be replaced by an open, and the op-amp is an inverting op-amp with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} G = -\frac{R_2}{R_1}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The cutoff frequency can now be calculated.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{\frac{1}{R_2C}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{R_2C}\right)^2 + \omega_c^2}}\\ \frac{1}{2} \amp= \frac{\left(\frac{1}{R_2C}\right)^2}{\left(\frac{1}{R_2C}\right)^2 + \omega_c^2}\\ \left(\frac{1}{R_2C}\right)^2 + \omega_c^2 \amp= 2\left(\frac{1}{R_2C}\right)^2\\ \omega_c^2 \amp= \left(\frac{1}{R_2C}\right)^2\\ \omega_c \amp= \frac{1}{R_2C} \end{align*}
    🔗
    To determine the filter type, first analyze the circuit at \(\omega=0\) to calculate \(v_{out}(0)\text{.}\) The capacitor can be replaced by an open.
    🔗
    Use (4.6.2) to calculate \(v_{out}(0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_{out}(0) = -\frac{R_2}{R_1}v_{in} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Analyze the circuit at \(\omega\rightarrow\infty\) to calculate \(v_{out}(\infty)\text{.}\) The capacitor can be replaced by a short.
    🔗
    The output voltage is zero due to the virtual node between the non-inverting and inverting inputs, and the fact that the shorted capacitor turns this into a voltage follower circuit (refer to Subsection 4.5.1).
    🔗
    Consult Table 9.3.31. The circuit is a first-order, active, low-pass filter.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.4.5 Active first-order high-pass filter.

    An active first-order high-pass filter is shown below. Calculate the cutoff frequency of the filter. Then, confirm the filter type of the circuit.
    🔗
    The transfer function for this circuit was derived in Example 9.2.7 and is given below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{-j\omega \frac{R_2}{R_1}}{j\omega + \frac{1}{R_1C}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To calculate the cutoff frequency, divide the transfer function by the gain of the circuit in the pass-band. Because this is a high-pass filter, the pass-band encompasses high frequencies. At high frequencies, the capacitor can be replaced by a short, and the op-amp is an inverting op-amp with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} G = -\frac{R_2}{R_1}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The cutoff frequency can now be calculated.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{\omega_c}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{R_1C}\right)^2 + \omega_c^2}}\\ \frac{1}{2} \amp= \frac{\omega_c^2}{\left(\frac{1}{R_1C}\right)^2 + \omega_c^2}\\ 2\omega_c^2 \amp= \left(\frac{1}{R_1C}\right)^2 + \omega_c^2\\ \omega_c^2 \amp= \left(\frac{1}{R_1C}\right)^2\\ \omega_c \amp= \frac{1}{R_1C} \end{align*}
    🔗
    To determine the filter type, first analyze the circuit at \(\omega=0\) to calculate \(v_{out}(0)\text{.}\) The capacitor can be replaced by an open.
    🔗
    The output is zero. The circuit in this condition is a voltage follower with the non-inverting input connected to ground.
    🔗
    Analyze the circuit at \(\omega\rightarrow\infty\) to calculate \(v_{out}(\infty)\text{.}\) The capacitor can be replaced by a short.
    🔗
    Use (4.6.2) to calculate \(v_{out}(\infty)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_{out}(\infty) = -\frac{R_2}{R_1}v_{in} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Consult Table 9.3.31. The circuit is a first-order, active, high-pass filter.

    Subsection 9.4.2 Cascaded Active Filters

    As mentioned in Subsection 4.6.3, op-amps can be cascaded together. While Subsection 4.6.3 made reference to the product of each individual circuit gain, cascaded filters can be analyzed by calculating the product of each filter’s transfer function. In this manner, band-pass filters, band-stop filters, and second-order (or higher) high-pass and low-pass filters can be designed.
    🔗
    Higher-order active filters can be created by cascading together multiple low-pass filters or multiple high-pass filters (for example). Cascading together identical filters creates a sharper slope at the cutoff frequency. An inverting amplifier can be staged at the output to create an overall circuit gain.
    🔗

    Example 9.4.9 Cascaded active band-pass filter.

    A low-pass filter whose input is fed into a high-pass filter acts as a band-pass filter. A cascaded active band-pass filter is depicted below. (Note that this is the circuit from Example 9.4.1 fed into the circuit from Example 9.4.5, whose output is then fed into an inverting op-amp.)
    🔗
    This filter has three stages. The first stage is a low-pass filter (analyzed in Example 9.4.1), the second stage is a high-pass filter (analyzed in Example 9.4.5), and the third stage is an inverting amplifier to provide overall amplification (gain).
    🔗
    The low-pass filter stage sets the upper cutoff frequency \(\omega_{c2}\text{.}\) Based on the analysis in Example 9.4.1, we can find that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \omega_{c2} = \frac{1}{R_LC_L}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The high-pass filter stage sets the lower cutoff frequency \(\omega_{c1}\text{.}\) Based on the analysis in Example 9.4.5, we can find that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \omega_{c1} = \frac{1}{R_HC_H}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The final stage has a gain given by (4.6.2) and sets the overall gain of the band-pass filter circuit. In this manner, each of the circuit properties can be tuned in each individual stage.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.4.3 Other Active Filter Topologies

    While there are numerous other active (and passive) filter designs that will not be explored in this book, one other active filter topology will be described. This is called the Sallen-Key topology. A low-pass Sallen-Key filter is depicted in Figure 9.4.13. (Swapping all resistors for capacitors and vice versa will result in a high-pass Sallen-Key filter. A band-pass Sallen-Key filter can also be realized.)
    🔗
    The voltage follower configuration of the circuit gives it a gain of one (unity gain), and ensures that the voltage at the non-inverting input of the op-amp is equal to \(v_{out}(t)\text{.}\) If a filter gain is desired, then the voltage follower configuration can be altered to include divided feedback instead of shorting the inverting input to the output.
    🔗
    The Sallen-Key filter shown in Figure 9.4.13 has a differential equation described in (9.4.1) and is derived in Derivation 9.4.3.1. The derivation uses KCL at the nodes connecting the two resistors to capacitor \(C_1\) and also at the non-inverting input of the op-amp.
    🔗
    \begin{align} \frac{1}{R_1R_2C_1C_2}v_{in}(t) \amp= \frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2}\tag{9.4.1}\\ \amp+ \left[\frac{1}{R_1C_1}+\frac{1}{R_2C_1}\right]\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\notag\\ \amp+ \left[\frac{1}{R_1R_2C_1C_2}\right]v_{out}(t)\notag \end{align}
    🔗

    Derivation.
    The term \(v_a(t)\) in this derivation denotes the node connecting the two resistors to capacitor \(C_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{v_{in}(t)-v_a(t)}{R_1} \amp= C_1\frac{d}{dt}\left[v_a(t)-v_{out}(t)\right] + C_2\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\\ \frac{v_{in}(t)}{R_1} \amp= \left[\frac{1}{R_1}+C_1\frac{d}{dt}\right]v_a(t) + \left[C_2\frac{d}{dt}-C_1\frac{d}{dt}\right]v_{out}(t)\\ \frac{v_a(t)-v_{out}(t)}{R_2} \amp= C_2\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\\ v_a(t) \amp= R_2C_2\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}+v_{out}(t)\\ \frac{v_{in}(t)}{R_1} \amp= \left[\frac{1}{R_1}+C_1\frac{d}{dt}\right]\left[R_2C_2\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}+v_{out}(t)\right]\\ \amp+ \left[C_2\frac{d}{dt}-C_1\frac{d}{dt}\right]v_{out}(t)\\ \amp= \frac{R_2C_2}{R_1}\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}+\frac{1}{R_1}v_{out}(t)+C_1R_2C_2\frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2}\\ \amp+ C_2\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\\ \amp= R_2C_1C_2\frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2}+\left[\frac{R_2C_2}{R_1}+C_2\right]\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\\ \amp+ \left[\frac{1}{R_1}\right]v_{out}(t)\\ \frac{1}{R_1R_2C_1C_2}v_{in}(t) \amp= \frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2}\\ \amp+ \left[\frac{1}{R_1C_1}+\frac{1}{R_2C_1}\right]\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\\ \amp+ \left[\frac{1}{R_1R_2C_1C_2}\right]v_{out}(t) \end{align*}
