    Section 9.3 Passive Filters

    A filter is a circuit that selectively blocks certain frequencies (or a range of frequencies) from passing through to the output. Filters can be passive (made of passive components only) or active (made with a combination of passive and active components, mostly commonly op-amps). Active filters will be discussed in Section 9.4.
    🔗
    Filters are defined by their frequency response. The four types of frequency responses are low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, and band-stop.
    🔗
    Filters are additionally defined by the circuit order. First-order filters consist of first-order circuits (only one energy storage device is included in the design). Second-order filters consist of second-order circuits (two energy storage devices are included in the design).
    🔗
    The cutoff frequency (\(\omega_c\)) of a filter defines the frequency at which input signals become modified by the circuit and either start to become blocked from or start to become passed to the output.
    🔗
    The cutoff frequency of a filter is defined by as the frequency where the magnitude of the transfer function of the filter is equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) relative to the magnitude of the transfer function in the pass-band, as described in (9.3.1). The pass-band defines the range of frequencies where the output signal is not blocked by the filter. For band-pass and band-stop filters, there will be two cutoff frequencies, denoted as \(\omega_{c1}\) and \(\omega_{c2}\text{.}\) (These two values will be defined in Subsection 9.3.3.) The unit of the cutoff frequency is rad/s.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} |H(\omega_c)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tag{9.3.1} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Remark 9.3.1 Summary of cutoff frequency.

    • Symbol: \(\omega_c\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Unit: rad/s (radians per second)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    A second-order filter is also characterized by a quantity known as the quality factor, \(Q\) (sometimes known as q-factor), which defines the “narrowness” of the filter response at the resonant frequency. A high quality factor means that the filter has a very narrow response (and vice versa). The quality factor of a second-order circuit is defined in (9.3.2), where \(\omega_0\) is the resonant frequency of the filter, and where \(a_1\) is one of the coefficients defined in (7.1.1) for a second-order differential equation in standard form.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} Q = \frac{\omega_0}{a_1}\tag{9.3.2} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Remark 9.3.2 Summary of quality factor.

    • Symbol: \(Q\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Unit: none
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Because the properties of a filter are dictated by the transfer function (in standard form) of the circuit, analysis of a filter will typically require deriving the corresponding transfer function. The process of deriving these equations will not be worked through in as much detail as it was in Section 9.2.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.1 Low-Pass Filters (LPF)

    A low-pass filter blocks high frequency signals (signals with a frequency above the cutoff frequency) from passing through to the output. (Only low frequencies can pass through, hence the name “low-pass filter.”) A common use of low-pass filters is to remove high frequency noise from a circuit’s output. The pass-band of a low-pass filter encompasses low frequencies. When calculating the cutoff frequency of a low-pass filter, the magnitude of the transfer function in the pass-band will be equal to \(|H(0)|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As the frequency of the input source changes, the magnitude of the output voltage will change. This can be characterized by calculating \(v_{out}/v_{in}\) at varying frequencies and looking at the response. An ideal low-pass filter has what’s called a “brick wall” response: all frequencies above the cutoff frequency are completely eliminated from the output, and all frequencies below the cutoff frequency are able to pass through with no change in magnitude.
    🔗
    The output response of a low-pass filter is shown in Figure 9.3.3. (Note the use of the log/log scale. In addition, note that the x-axis is normalized to the cutoff frequency.) The ideal brick wall response is depicted with a dashed line. The real response expected from a low-pass filter is depicted with a thick black line.
    🔗
    A second-order low-pass filter will cause a much faster decay of the output signal compared to a first-order low-pass filter. A comparison of waveforms for a first and second-order low-pass filter (with identical cutoff frequencies) is shown in Figure 9.3.4.

    Example 9.3.8 Low-pass filter analysis.

    Analyze the following low-pass filter. Calculate the cutoff frequency and quality factor of the filter. The circuit parameters are: \(R=500~\Omega\text{,}\) \(L=1~\textrm{H}\text{,}\) and \(C=100~\mu\textrm{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The filter is a second-order low-pass filter. This is because the circuit contains two separate energy-storage elements.
    🔗
    The second-order differential equation will provide information about the properties of the circuit. Perform KCL at the node connecting the inductor, resistor, and capacitor. Then, reduce to standard form.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{1}{L}\int(v_{in}(t) - v_{out}(t)) \,dt \amp= \frac{v_{out}(t)}{R} + C\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\\ \frac{1}{L}(v_{in}(t) - v_{out}(t)) \amp= \frac{1}{R}\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt} + C\frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2}\\ \frac{1}{L}v_{in}(t) \amp= C\frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{R}\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}v_{out}(t)\\ \frac{1}{LC}v_{in}(t) \amp= \frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{RC}\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}v_{out}(t) \end{align*}
    🔗
    The transfer function of the circuit is therefore equal to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{\frac{1}{LC}}{-\omega^2 + \frac{1}{RC}j\omega + \frac{1}{LC}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Plug in component values using base units.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{10000}{-\omega^2 + 20j\omega + 10000} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Calculate the magnitude of the transfer function.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega)| \amp= \Big|\frac{\frac{1}{LC}}{-\omega^2 + \frac{1}{RC}j\omega + \frac{1}{LC}}\Big|\\ \amp= \frac{\frac{1}{LC}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{LC}-\omega^2\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{RC}\omega\right)^2}} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Calculate the resonant frequency of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_0 \amp= \sqrt{10000}\\ \amp= 100~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (9.3.2) to calculate the quality factor of the filter.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} Q \amp= \frac{100}{20}\\ \amp= 5 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Calculate the magnitude of the transfer function in the pass-band. (I.E. \(|H(0)|\text{.}\))
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(0)| \amp= \frac{\frac{1}{LC}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{LC}-0\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{RC}(0)\right)^2}}\\ \amp= \frac{\frac{1}{LC}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{LC}\right)^2}}\\ \amp= \frac{\frac{1}{LC}}{\frac{1}{LC}}\\ \amp= 1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Set \(|H(j\omega)|\) equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) and solve for \(\omega\) to calculate the cutoff frequency of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega_c)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{a_0}{\sqrt{(a_0-\omega_c^2)^2 + (a_1\omega_c)^2}}\\ 2a_0^2 \amp= (a_0-\omega_c^2)^2 + (a_1\omega_c)^2\\ \amp= a_1^2\omega_c^2 + (a_0-\omega_c^2)^2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Let \(x = \omega_c^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 2a_0^2 \amp= a_1^2x + (a_0-x)^2\\ \amp= a_1^2x + a_0^2 - 2a_0x + x^2\\ 0 \amp= x^2 + (a_1^2 - 2a_0)x - a_0^2\\ \amp= x^2 - 19600x - (10000)^2\\ \omega_c \amp= 154~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The Bode plot for this filter is shown below.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.2 High-Pass Filters (HPF)

    A high-pass filter blocks low frequency signals (signals with a frequency below the cutoff frequency) from passing through to the output. (Only high frequencies can pass through, hence the name “high-pass filter.”) The pass-band of a high-pass filter encompasses high frequencies. When calculating the cutoff frequency of a high-pass filter, the magnitude of the transfer function in the pass-band will be equal to \(|H(\infty)|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As the frequency of the input source changes, the magnitude of the output voltage will change. This can be characterized by calculating \(v_{out}/v_{in}\) at varying frequencies and looking at the response. An ideal high-pass filter has an ideal brick wall response where all frequencies below the cutoff frequency are completely eliminated from the output, and all frequencies above the cutoff frequency are able to pass through with no change in magnitude.

    The output response of a high-pass filter is shown in Figure 9.3.11. (Note the use of the log/log scale. In addition, note that the x-axis is normalized to the cutoff frequency.)     A second-order high-pass filter will cause a much faster rise of the output signal compared to a first-order high-pass filter. A comparison of waveforms for a first and second-order high-pass filter (with identical cutoff frequencies) is shown in Figure 9.3.12.

    Example 9.3.16 High-pass filter analysis.

    Analyze the following high-pass filter. Calculate the cutoff frequency and quality factor of the filter. The circuit parameters are: \(C = 200~\textrm{nF}\text{,}\) \(R_1 = 500~\Omega\text{,}\) \(R_2 = 400~\Omega\text{,}\) and \(L = 10~\textrm{mH}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The second-order differential equation describing the output voltage is shown below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d^2v_{in}(t)}{dt^2} = \frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2} + \left[\frac{1}{CR_1} + \frac{R_2}{L}\right]\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt} + \left[\frac{R_2}{R_1LC} + \frac{1}{LC}\right]v_{out}(t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The transfer function of the circuit is therefore equal to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{-\omega^2}{-\omega^2 + \left[\frac{1}{CR_1} + \frac{R_2}{L}\right]j\omega + \left[\frac{R_2}{R_1LC} + \frac{1}{LC}\right]}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Plug in component values using base units.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{-\omega^2}{-\omega^2 + 50000j\omega + 900000000} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Calculate the magnitude of the transfer function.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega)| \amp= \Big| \frac{-\omega^2}{-\omega^2 + \left[\frac{1}{CR_1} + \frac{R_2}{L}\right]j\omega + \left[\frac{R_2}{R_1LC} + \frac{1}{LC}\right]}\Big|\\ \amp= \frac{\omega^2}{\sqrt{\left(\left[\frac{R_2}{R_1LC} + \frac{1}{LC}\right]-\omega^2\right)^2 + \left(\left[\frac{1}{CR_1} + \frac{R_2}{L}\right]\omega\right)^2}} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Calculate the resonant frequency of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_0 \amp= \sqrt{900000000}\\ \amp= 30000~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (9.3.2) to calculate the quality factor of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} Q \amp= \frac{30000}{50000}\\ \amp= 0.6 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Note that at sufficiently large values of \(\omega\text{,}\) the magnitude of the transfer function becomes equal to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\omega^2}{\omega^2} = 1\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This means that the magnitude of the transfer function in the pass-band is equal to one.
    🔗
    Set \(|H(j\omega)|\) equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) and solve for \(\omega\) to calculate the cutoff frequency of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega_c)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{\omega_c^2}{\sqrt{(a_0-\omega_c^2)^2 + (a_1\omega_c)^2}}\\ 2(\omega_c^2)^2 \amp= (a_0-\omega_c^2)^2 + (a_1\omega_c)^2\\ \amp= a_1^2\omega_c^2 + (a_0-\omega_c^2)^2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Let \(x = \omega_c^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 2x^2 \amp= a_1^2x + (a_0-x)^2\\ \amp= a_1^2x + a_0^2 - 2a_0x + x^2\\ 0 \amp= x^2 + (2a_0 - a_1^2)x - a_0^2\\ \amp= x^2 -700000000x - (900000000)^2\\ \omega_c \amp= 36300~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The Bode plot for this filter is shown below.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.3 Band-Pass and Band-Stop Filters

    Before going into the details of each individual filter in Subsection 9.3.4 and Subsection 9.3.5, it is useful to introduce, describe, and derive aspects that are common to both of these two filter types.
    🔗
    Band-pass filters and band-stop filters are defined by their ability to pass or stop a given range of frequencies from the output, respectively. The width of the pass-band or stop-band is defined by the two cutoff frequencies (\(\omega_{c1}\) and \(\omega_{c2}\)), which are the two frequencies where the magnitude of the transfer function is equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\text{.}\) The bandwidth (\(\beta\)) of a band-pass filter or band-stop filter is therefore defined by (9.3.3). (Note that while bandwidth shares a symbol with the oscillation frequency of a second-order circuit, the two quantities are not the same!)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \beta = \omega_{c2}-\omega_{c1}\tag{9.3.3} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Remark 9.3.19 Summary of bandwidth.

    • Symbol: \(\beta\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Unit: rad/s (radians per second)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The bandwidth for an RLC band-pass filter and RLC band-stop filter can be calculated using their respective transfer functions. This equation is defined in (9.3.4), and derived in Derivation 9.3.3.1 for an RLC band-pass filter and in Derivation 9.3.3.2 for an RLC band-stop filter.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \beta = a_1\tag{9.3.4} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Derivation.
    The transfer function of an RLC band-pass filter will take the form shown below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{a_1 j\omega}{-\omega^2 + a_1 j\omega + a_0} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The magnitude of this transfer function is shown below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |H(j\omega)| = \frac{(a_1 \omega)}{\sqrt{(a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Set the magnitude of the transfer function equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) and solve for the two values of \(\omega\) that are positive. These will describe the two cutoff frequencies \(\omega_{c1}\) and \(\omega_{c2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{a_1 \omega}{\sqrt{(a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2}}\\ \frac{1}{2} \amp= \frac{(a_1 \omega)^2}{(a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2}\\ 2(a_1 \omega )^2 \amp= (a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2\\ (a_0 - \omega^2)^2 \amp= (a_1 \omega)^2\\ 0 \amp= a_0^2 - 2a_0\omega^2 + \omega^4 - a_1^2 \omega^2 \\ \amp= \omega^4 - (2a_0 + a_1^2) \omega^2 + a_0^2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    The two positive real roots are shown below. These will not be derived in this book.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_{c1} \amp= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} - a_1\right)\\ \omega_{c2} \amp= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} + a_1\right) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Subtract \(\omega_{c1}\) from \(\omega_{c2}\) to calculate the bandwidth.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \beta \amp= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} + a_1\right) - \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} - a_1\right)\\ \amp= \frac{1}{2}a_1 + \frac{1}{2}a_1\\ \amp= a_1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Derivation.
    The transfer function of an RLC band-stop filter will take the form shown below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{-\omega^2 + a_0}{-\omega^2 + a_1 j\omega + a_0} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The magnitude of this transfer function is shown below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |H(j\omega)| = \frac{(a_0 - \omega^2)}{\sqrt{(a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Set the magnitude of the transfer function equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) and solve for the two values of \(\omega\) that are positive. These will describe the two cutoff frequencies \(\omega_{c1}\) and \(\omega_{c2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{(a_0 - \omega^2)}{\sqrt{(a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2}}\\ \frac{1}{2} \amp= \frac{(a_0 - \omega^2)^2}{(a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2}\\ 2(a_0 - \omega^2)^2 \amp= (a_0-\omega^2)^2 + (a_1 \omega)^2\\ (a_0 - \omega^2)^2 \amp= (a_1 \omega)^2\\ 0 \amp= a_0^2 - 2a_0\omega^2 + \omega^4 - a_1^2 \omega^2 \\ \amp= \omega^4 - (2a_0 + a_1^2) \omega^2 + a_0^2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    The two positive real roots are shown below. These will not be derived in this book.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_{c1} \amp= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} - a_1\right)\\ \omega_{c2} \amp= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} + a_1\right) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Subtract \(\omega_{c1}\) from \(\omega_{c2}\) to calculate the bandwidth.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \beta \amp= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} + a_1\right) - \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{a_1^2+4a_0} - a_1\right)\\ \amp= \frac{1}{2}a_1 + \frac{1}{2}a_1\\ \amp= a_1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Band-pass and band-stop filters, as mentioned, have two cutoff frequencies straddling a center frequency. The center frequency of a band-pass or band-stop filter is equal to the resonant frequency of the circuit, as defined in (9.3.5).
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \omega_c = \omega_0\tag{9.3.5} \end{equation}

    Subsection 9.3.4 Band-Pass Filters (BPF)

    A band-pass filter only allows a range of frequencies to pass through to the output. Anything below a certain frequency, or above a certain frequency, is blocked from reaching the output. The range of frequencies that are allowed to pass through to the output is known as the pass-band of the circuit. The width of the pass-band is defined by the bandwidth of the circuit. Band-pass filters cannot be first-order circuits; they must be second-order or higher.
    🔗
    As the frequency of the input source changes, the magnitude of the output voltage will change. This can be characterized by calculating \(H(j\omega)\) at varying frequencies and looking at the response. An ideal band-pass filter has an ideal brick wall response where all frequencies outside the pass-band are completely eliminated from the output, and all frequencies inside the pass-band are able to pass through with no change in magnitude.
    🔗
    The output response of a band-pass filter is shown in Figure 9.3.21. (Note the use of the log/log scale. In addition, note that the x-axis is normalized to the cutoff frequency.) The ideal brick wall response is depicted with a dashed line. The real response expected from a band-pass filter is depicted with a thick black line.
    🔗
    Band-pass filters are characterized by their quality factor. A filter with a high quality factor will have a very narrow pass-band (low bandwidth), and vice versa. A graph showing the output response of band-pass filters with the same center frequency but with different quality factors is shown in Figure 9.3.22.

    Example 9.3.23 Band-pass filter analysis.

    Analyze the following band-pass filter. Calculate the center frequency and the quality factor of the circuit. The circuit parameters are: \(C_1 = 40~\mu\textrm{F}\text{,}\) \(C_2 = 20~\mu\textrm{F}\text{,}\) \(R_1 = 200~\Omega\text{,}\) and \(R_2 = 120~\Omega\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The second-order differential equation describing the output voltage is shown below.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{1}{R_2C_2}\frac{dv_{in}(t)}{dt} \amp= \frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2} + \left[\frac{1}{C_1R_1} + \frac{1}{C_2R_2} + \frac{1}{C_1R_2}\right]\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt}\\ \amp+ \left[\frac{1}{C_1C_2R_1R_2}\right]v_{out}(t) \end{align*}
    🔗
    The transfer function of the circuit is given below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{\frac{1}{R_2C_2} j\omega}{-\omega^2 + \left[\frac{1}{C_1R_1} + \frac{1}{C_2R_2} + \frac{1}{C_1R_2}\right]j\omega + \left[\frac{1}{C_1C_2R_1R_2}\right]} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Plug in component values using base units.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{416.6667 j\omega}{-\omega^2 + 750 j\omega + 52083.33} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Use (9.3.5) to calculate the center frequency of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_c \amp= \sqrt{52083.33}\\ \amp= 228~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (9.3.2) to calculate the quality factor of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} Q \amp= \frac{228}{750}\\ \amp= 0.304 \end{align*}
    🔗
    There is no value of the magnitude of the transfer function that is equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\text{.}\) Therefore, it’s not possible to directly calculate the cutoff frequencies of this circuit. However, this circuit is built from two stages. The first stage (consisting of resistor \(R_1\) and capacitor \(C_1\)) is a high-pass filter. The second stage (consisting of resistor \(R_2\) and capacitor \(C_2\)) is a low-pass filter. The cutoff frequency of each of these two stages can be calculated.
    🔗
    Stage one is a HPF with a transfer function defined below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{j\omega}{j\omega + \frac{1}{R_1C_1}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The magnitude of this transfer function is equal to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |H(j\omega)| = \frac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_1C_1}\right)^2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Set \(|H(j\omega)|\) equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) and solve for \(\omega\) to calculate the cutoff frequency of stage one. This will be one of the two cutoff frequencies of the band-pass filter.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega_c)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{\omega_c}{\sqrt{\omega_c^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_1C_1}\right)^2}}\\ \frac{1}{2} \amp= \frac{\omega_c^2}{\omega_c^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_1C_1}\right)^2}\\ \omega_c^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_1C_1}\right)^2 \amp= 2\omega_c^2\\ \omega_c^2 \amp= \left(\frac{1}{R_1C_1}\right)^2\\ \omega_c \amp= \frac{1}{R_1C_1}\\ \amp= \frac{1}{(200)(40 \times 10^{-6})}\\ \amp= 125~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Stage two is a LPF with a transfer function defined below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{\frac{1}{R_2C_2}}{j\omega + \frac{1}{R_2C_2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The magnitude of this transfer function is equal to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |H(j\omega)| = \frac{\frac{1}{R_2C_2}}{\sqrt{\omega^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_2C_2}\right)^2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Set \(|H(j\omega)|\) equal to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) and solve for \(\omega\) to calculate the cutoff frequency of stage two. This will be the second of the two cutoff frequencies of the band-pass filter.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} |H(j\omega_c)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp= \frac{\frac{1}{R_2C_2}}{\sqrt{\omega_c^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_2C_2}\right)^2}}\\ \frac{1}{2} \amp= \frac{\left(\frac{1}{R_2C_2}\right)^2}{\omega_c^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_2C_2}\right)^2}\\ \omega_c^2 + \left(\frac{1}{R_2C_2}\right)^2 \amp= 2\left(\frac{1}{R_2C_2}\right)^2\\ \omega_c^2 \amp= \left(\frac{1}{R_2C_2}\right)^2\\ \omega_c \amp= \frac{1}{R_2C_2}\\ \amp= \frac{1}{(120)(20 \times 10^{-6})}\\ \amp= 417~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The Bode plot for this circuit is shown below. The Bode plots of each stages are shown in dashed lines to make it clear how the overall transfer function relates to the transfer functions of each stage.

    Subsection 9.3.5 Band-Stop Filters (BSF)

    A band-stop filter only allows a range of frequencies to pass through to the output. Anything between a range of frequencies known as the stop-band is blocked from reaching the output. All other frequencies are allowed through. The width of the stop-band is defined by the bandwidth of the circuit. Band-stop filters cannot be first-order circuits; they must be second-order or higher.
    🔗
    As the frequency of the input source changes, the magnitude of the output voltage will change. This can be characterized by calculating \(v_{out}/v_{in}\) at varying frequencies and looking at the response. An ideal band-stop filter has an ideal brick wall response where all frequencies inside the stop-band are completely eliminated from the output, and all frequencies outside the stop-band are able to pass through with no change in magnitude.
    🔗
    The output response of a band-stop filter is shown in Figure 9.3.26. (Note the use of the log/log scale. In addition, note that the x-axis is normalized to the cutoff frequency.) The ideal brick wall response is depicted with a dashed line. The real response expected from a band-stop filter is depicted with a thick black line.
    🔗
    Band-stop filters are characterized by their quality factor. A filter with a high quality factor will have a very narrow stop-band (low bandwidth), and vice versa. A graph showing the output response of band-stop filters with the same center frequency but with different quality factors is shown in Figure 9.3.27.
    🔗

    Example 9.3.28 Band-stop filter analysis.

    Analyze the following band-stop filter. Calculate the center frequency, the bandwidth, cutoff frequencies, and the quality factor of the circuit. The circuit parameters are: \(R = 10~\Omega\text{,}\) \(L = 200~\textrm{mH}\text{,}\) and \(C = 50~\mu\textrm{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The second-order differential equation describing the output voltage is shown below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d^2v_{in}(t)}{dt^2} +\frac{1}{LC}v_{in}(t)= \frac{d^2v_{out}(t)}{dt^2} + \left[\frac{R}{L}\right]\frac{dv_{out}(t)}{dt} + \left[\frac{1}{LC}\right]v_{out}(t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The transfer function of the circuit is therefore equal to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{-\omega^2 + \frac{1}{LC}}{-\omega^2 + \frac{R}{L}j\omega + \frac{1}{LC}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Plug in component values using base units.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(j\omega) = \frac{-\omega^2 + 100000}{-\omega^2 + 50j\omega + 100000}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Use (9.3.5) to calculate the center frequency of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_c \amp= \sqrt{100000}\\ \amp= 316~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (9.3.2) to calculate the quality factor of the circuit.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} Q \amp= \frac{316~\textrm{rad/s}}{50~\textrm{rad/s}}\\ \amp= 6.32 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Use (9.3.4) to calculate the bandwidth of the filter.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \beta = 50~\textrm{rad/s} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The two cutoff frequencies can be calculated directly from the center frequency and bandwidth.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \omega_{c1}, \omega_{c2} \amp= \omega_0 \pm \frac{\beta}{2}\\ \omega_{c1} \amp= \omega_0 - \frac{\beta}{2}\\ \amp= 316~\textrm{rad/s} - 25~\textrm{rad/s}\\ \amp= 291~\textrm{rad/s}\\ \omega_{c2} \amp= \omega_0 + \frac{\beta}{2}\\ \amp= 316~\textrm{rad/s} + 25~\textrm{rad/s}\\ \amp= 341~\textrm{rad/s} \end{align*}

    The Bode plot for this filter is shown below.

    Subsection 9.3.6 Determining Filter Type

    To determine the type of filter a circuit corresponds to, first analyze the circuit in a DC steady-state condition (\(\omega=0\)). In this case, all capacitors can be replaced by opens and all inductors can be replaced by shorts. Determine the value of the output voltage (which will either be zero or non-zero).
    🔗
    Second, analyze the circuit given a frequency approaching infinity (\(\omega\rightarrow\infty\)). In this case, all capacitors can be replaced by shorts and all inductors can be replaced by opens. Determine the value of the output voltage (which will either be zero or non-zero).
    🔗
    Then, use Table 9.3.31 to determine the filter type based on these two calculations.
    🔗
