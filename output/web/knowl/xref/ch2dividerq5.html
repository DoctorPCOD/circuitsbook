Exercise 15.

Use the voltage divider rule to calculate \(V_{X1}\) and \(V_{X2}\) in the circuit shown in Figure 2.10.35.
🔗
Answer.
\begin{align*} V_{X1} \amp= V_S\left(\frac{R_3}{R_1+R_2+R_3}\right)\\ V_{X2} \amp= V_S\left[\frac{R_7}{R_6+R_7}\right]\left[\frac{R_5//(R_6+R_7)}{R_4+R_5//(R_6+R_7)}\right] \end{align*}
🔗
🔗
Solution.
The two “halves” of the circuit can be treated separately from each other. This is because the two halves are in parallel, and, from a standpoint of voltage, have identical voltage drops. First, let’s consider the left “half,” re-drawn below.
🔗
All of the resistors are in series with the voltage source, so the voltage divider rule can be applied directly to calculate \(V_{X1}\text{.}\)
🔗
\begin{equation*} V_{X1} = V_S\left(\frac{R_3}{R_1+R_2+R_3}\right) \end{equation*}
🔗
Now, let’s consider the right “half,”, re-drawn below.
🔗
To calculate \(V_{X2}\text{,}\) the process used in Exercise 2.10.13 will be used again. Be sure you are comfortable with that process before proceeding, as not every step will be exhaustively recounted in this solution.
🔗
The voltage drop over resistor \(R_5\) can be used to calculate \(V_{X2}\text{.}\) To calculate that voltage drop, we must first calculate the equivalent resistance seen by \(R_5\text{.}\)
🔗
\begin{equation*} R_{EQ,R5} = R_5//(R_6+R_7) \end{equation*}
🔗
Now, use the voltage divider rule to calculate the voltage drop over this equivalent resistance.
🔗
\begin{align*} V_{R5} \amp= V_S\left(\frac{R_{EQ,R5}}{R_4+R_{EQ,R5}}\right)\\ \amp= V_S\left(\frac{R_5//(R_6+R_7)}{R_4+R_5//(R_6+R_7)}\right) \end{align*}
🔗
Now, use the voltage divider rule with \(V_{R5}\) as the source voltage to calculate \(V_{X2}\text{.}\)
🔗
\begin{align*} V_{X2} \amp= V_{R5}\left(\frac{R_7}{R_6+R_7}\right)\\ \amp= V_S\left[\frac{R_7}{R_6+R_7}\right]\left[\frac{R_5//(R_6+R_7)}{R_4+R_5//(R_6+R_7)}\right] \end{align*}
